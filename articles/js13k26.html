A devouring passion for words

I've been quite passionate this year, but my biggest special interest by far been WORDS !!!

More precisely, I wanted to gather good dictionaries, see how small I could compress them, and eventually use them in JS games...
which led to the creation of the following projects:

  • Words is a webpage displaying 3 different french and english dictionaries in many formats and with a lot of options (Github - Website)
  • MiniPrefixRemover is a first attempt at compressing an array of words by removing repeated prefixes (Github - Website)
  • Boggle is a Boggle grid generator and solver based on the Scrabble dictionaries from "words" (Github - Website)
  • Word Games is my bonus GamedevJS 2026 entry, coded in 24h: five mini daily word games, also based on "words" (Github - Website)

And then, I thought about JS13kGames...

How cool would it be if I could find a way to compress a big list of words as strongly as possible, and embed it in a js13kGames entry?
Let it be my compression challenge this year!

But for once, I didn't do it alone. I asked AIs for assistance. 😱

A fair bit of warning here:
It's not AI slop: I didn't let an AI run for days until it found the best possible compression. That would have ruined all the fun!
It's not really vibe-coding either, because no AI-generated code will be present in the final game.
What I did instead, I'd call it vibe-brainstorming.

First, I played with "words", "MiniPrefixRemover" and "RoadRoller" to identify the best candidate for my eventual "js13k dictionary":
My choice was the following: take all the English Scrabble words, up to 5 letters long.
That's a list of 14,915 words, weighing 109kb in JSON format or 80kb in a comma-separated string.
Zipping this list leads to a 24kb zip file...
Or as little as 14kb if we do a strong pass of RoadRoller before zipping...
Super impressive, but still too big.

But if I try again with MiniPrefixRemover, then RoadRoller, then zip, now we're under 13kb (12,741b)!
That's very nice, but not enough to include a nice word game, even less a collection of 5 word games, as I did in my GamedevJS entry...

So how did the vibe-brainstorming work? Quite obsessively, I'd say:
I spent the last 6 months chatting pretty much daily with ChatGPT (until it asked me to pay), then Claude (until it asked me to pay), then Gemini, in one unique goal:
Make my dictionary fit in the smallest possible zip file, ideally under 11kb.

Six month of discussion about compressing a dictionary, that's long. How long?
Well, just to give you an idea, I copy-pasted all my exchanges in a Word document with a 12pt font-size, and it exceeded 1500 pages.
About 75% of the exchanges are HTML code (because we coded many tools along the way), but there's also a lot of dialog.

Here's another way to see it: I made a video showing my conversation (in french) with the 3 AIs:

How I brainstormed with these AIs:

As I said, I didn't ask the AIs to do everything for me.
I sent them my idea, and only sometimes, asked their help to formalize my ideas, implement them, or suggest improvements.

Every time I had a (more or less crazy) new idea to compress my words, I explained that idea to the AI and asked it to code me a little HTML lab: a webpage that loads the dictionary, applies all the transformations I want, roadroll and zip the result, and check if the encoded data is 1) decodable without ambiguity, and 2) smaller that our current record.

So technically, these HTML labs are vibe-coded, but they're not part of a game, only tools used to generate a compressed dictionary.

I'm pretty sure we covered virtually every possible compression approach currently known on Earth, from arithmetic encoding to woff2 brotli embedding, with long detours towards tries, RLE, SSE, ZSDT, DAWG, BWT, bigrams, trigrams, lexical pruning, column delta, and many others...

The main thing that I learned from the vast majority of these approaches is that they introduced entropy in my data, making it actually less RoadRoller-friendly and less Zip-friendly. As a result, my zip's size grew instead of shrining. That's sad... But also very logical.

Since RoadRoller and Zip LOVE low-entropy data (data that is often repeated and/or easily predictable), the only viable solution was to transform my dictionary, but juuuuust a little:

  • Omitting repeated prefixes (we saw earlier that it was beneficial)
  • Introducing a few special characters to help eliminate huge chunks of redundant information
  • Nudging, reformatting and reordering things everywhere
... the secret is to NEVER make the data less predictable by trying to be "clever":
No binary code, no random-ish noise, nothing that RoadRoller or Gzip already does afterwards.

When I understood that, my "dumb" suggestions finally became the "clever" ones, and we finally started reducing my zip's size!

Starting from my 12.7kb record, I found a combination of 5 techniques that progressively improved the compression of my dictionary.
These techniques are implemented in my Wordcompression Github repo and explained below:

The AIs were mostly able to suggest well-known compression algorithms, which were inefficient in this context.
As a result, the 5 "recipes" that we ended up using have been 100% invented by me.
The AIs were there to formalize my ideas, implement the HTML labs, and explain to me why some things worked and some other things didn't work.
Though, I recognize that they've been a great help, without ruining the fun of thinking and "having my hands inside the engine" the whole time.
I now consider that it's how AIs should be used: as brainstorming buddies and quick prototypers, but not as game makers. (that's my job!)

What's the final dictionary size?

On july 27 2026, I ended up reducing it under 11kb (11,266 bytes), thanks to our last optimizer called "shuffler2.html".

How did we manage to fit 14,915 words under 11kb?

That's the most interesting part, so let's watch it interactively, starting with the raw words list!

(Note: as they come from the Scrabble dictionary, these words are 2-5 letters long, ordered alphabetically, lowercase, and don't contain accents, apostrophes or hyphens. In other words, they only require 26 different characters: a to z.)

Dictionary optimization

You can click the buttons below to transform the list into its final form, and unroll each step to read the full explanations.

(Raw size: 82,751 bytes - Zip size: 13,965 bytes)

Raw list (open for details)
14,915 words, comma-separated. 2 letters: 107 words. 3 letters: 1085 words. 4 letters: 4247 words. 5 letters: 9476 words.
Note: it keeps increasing up to 8 letters: 6 letters: 16,706 words. 7 letters: 25,473 words. 8 letters: 31,736 words. 9+ letters: 107,771.
Raw size: 82,751 bytes.
Roadroller + zip: 13,965 bytes.

Replace prefixes with their length
Use the digits 0-4 to represent the size of the prefix repeated from the last word.
(Ex: "aa, aah, aahed, aahs" → "aa2h3ed3s")
Raw size: 35,262 bytes (removed 61,403 chars + added 14,914 digits)
Roadroller + zip: 12,741 bytes.

Replace plurals with a 9
Use the digit 9 to replace words that only add an "s" to a previous word
(Ex: "aa, aah, aahed, aahs, aal, aalii, aals, aargh, aas" → "aa92h93ed2l93ii2rgh")
Note: the digit 9 is placed right after the singular word, even if the plural was not immediately following (like "aa" and "aas").
Note 2: sometimes, words "with an extra s" are not exactly plurals (like has, his, yes, news, hiss, brass, pass, buss, puss).
Of course, they still get replaced with a 9, like the others. The goal here is compression, not semantics ;)
Note 3: I tried replacing other suffixes than "s" with dedicated digits, but they all made the zip become bigger.
Raw size: 31,811 bytes (3,590 plurals replaced with a 9).
Roadroller + zip: 11,585 bytes.

Remap the alphabet
Rewrite and reorder all the words with an alphabet that favorizes the most frequent letters.
(abcdefghijklmnopqrstuvwxyz → esaoriltnudpcmyhgbkfwvzxjq)
Note: the dictionary is ordered alphabetically after the remap, so the first words in the list ("aaau2efa...") now correspond to "eeew, eerie, ...".
Note 2: The remap string will have to be hardcoded in the dictionary decoder, but it's fine: it will be useful for the word games too!
Raw size: 31,811 bytes (no change, it's only a zip optimization)
Roadroller + zip: 11,447 bytes.

Shuffle the 26 word blocks
Remapping the alphabet already reordered the dictionary a little, but randomly shuffling blocks of words based on their first letter can also help the zip lose extra bytes.
Brute-forcing the search for an optimal order would have taken more time than the age of the universe, so we went with a stochastic approach (try stuff randomly and only keep the branches where an improvement actually happens).
The most efficient order found using this technique is: prqlgitnevxhkuzbmscfdajowy.
Raw size: 31,811 bytes.
Roadroller + zip: 11,335 bytes.

Shuffle everything!
Why stop there? Let's do a "5D shuffler" that takes all the words that share the same prefix length and shuffle them optimally.
It shuffles the 26 "zero-level" blocks, then in each block, it shuffles all the sub-blocks where the prefix length equals 1.
Then in every sub-block, it shuffles all the sub-sub-blocks where the prefix length equals 2, etc...
And when the "prefix length equals 4" blocks are optimally shuffled, we finally shuffle all the individual words inside them.
Raw size: 31,811 bytes.
Roadroller + zip: 11,201 bytes.

And just like that, right before the start of the compo, I managed to create a 14,915 words dictionary that fits in 10.9kb !
I was flabbergasted to discover that this optimization basically encodes each word (initially weighing 2-5 bytes) in about 0,75 byte (6 bits).
I'm super proud of this achievement, but what's even cooler is that now I have enough space to include 5 word games for JS13K26 !

Days 3-9: title screen and intro

More and more certain that my game will contain lots of animations, and that I won't have the full month free for coding, I started with the title screen (a magic island made of CSS3D and emoji), and the real game's introduction sequence (where the magic crayons disappear from the island). The crayons are made in SVG. For simplicity, the unicorn will be made from emojis and SVG instead of letting the player draw it.

Title screen progression:




The CSS3D scene and animations are made with my CSS3Dframework library.
The SVGs are made with my MiniSVGCreator tool.
The rainbow is inspired by the one I did for my JS13k25 entry I GOT UP ON THE WRONG FOOT.

For the intro, the idea was to see the crayons disappear and the island becomes gray and sad. I also did it with CSS3D.



The intro is much darker than I had anticipated on days 1 and 2.
When I made it, I thought it would create an interesting contrast with the whimsical theme and title screen.
Some testers remarked the irony of the fire appearing under the rain, that was intentional.

Fun fact: We're only a few seconds in-game and my entry has already used 4 different rendering techniques (Emoji, CSS3D, SVG and 2D canvas).
CSS3D is used on the title screen, and stops being used when the fire starts (CSS is super useful for all the animations happening there).
From the dead tree, up to the end of the game, everything is rendered on a 2D canvas (on 4 canvases, actually: one for the background, one for the rain, one for the drawing board and one for the actual drawings), plus CSS gradients for the sky and god rays, and HTML buttons when necessary.
After the title screen, my code was 12kb commented, and 2.6kb zipped.
After the intro, my code was 19.5kb, and 3.3kb zipped, including rain and thunder sound.

When we arrive to the first drawing board (after the dead tree and sad unicorn face), the commented code is 23kb, and 4kb zipped.

The emoji webfont used in the final version is different from the games I made between 2017 and 2025: this year I found that Twemoji was too pixellized AND out-of-date.
On the other hand, Google's Noto Color Emoji supported vectorial emoji on all browsers and Unicode 17.0.
Switching to this font (and hosting it for easy reuse) has been a great help for improving my entry's graphics.

Alas, this year has also been the scene for The Great Emoji Font Drama...
To summarize, in the Alkor and End3r started enforcing the js13k rule that says "webfont polyfills can be loaded with the authorization of the player", mid-jam.

All the devs who had started using Twemoji or Noto Color Emoji for free, as they did the years before, were suddenly seen as some kind of cheaters, and were asked to add a screen asking for the player's consent.
My entry, who loaded the Noto Color Emoji *AND* Comic Sans MS (which I both consider "webfont polyfills", as they're not natively present on every OS) was the most frowned upon, and I had to free a few hundred bytes, ask consent for the emoji font, remove the comic sans MS font entirely, all that that while being on holidays 1,400km from my home.


That was really not fun. I wasn't trying to cheat anyone, just making the best possible experience for everyone.
I hope the process for allowing emoji polyfills will be clearer and more dev-friendly before the 2027 edition.
IMO, the emoji font(s) should be served by js13kgames.com and useable for free in any entry.
Disclosing the use of such fonts could be simplified by checking a dedicated checkbox in the submission form, which would automatically write an "external font used" mention in the entry's page.
