And then, I thought about JS13kGames...

How cool would it be if I could find a way to compress a big list of words as strongly as possible, and embed it in a js13kGames entry?
Let it be my compression challenge this year!

But for once, I didn't do it alone. I asked AIs for assistance.
But a fair bit of warning here: I didn't let an AI run for days until it found the best possible compression. That would have ruined all the fun!
It's not really vibe-coding either, because no AI code will be present in the final product.
What I did instead, I'd call it vibe-brainstorming and vibe-experimenting.

First, I played with "words", "MiniPrefixRemover" and "RoadRoller" to identify the best candidate for my "js13k dictionary":
Let's take all English Scrabble words, up to 5 letters long. That's a list of 14,915 words, weighing 109kb in JSON format or 80kb in a comma-separated string.
Zipping this list leads to a 24kb zip file... or 14kb ony if we add a strong pass of RoadRoller before zipping. Super impressive, but still too big.
But if I try again with MiniPrefixRemover, then RoadRoller, then zip, now we're under 13kb (12,741b)!
That's very nice, but not enough to include a nice word game, or a collection of 5 word games similar to my GamedevJS entry...
(and yeah, since my words are 5 letters long, I'd like to include 5 word games in total, so my entry revolves around the number five. If that's possible).

So how did the vibe-brainstorming work? Quite obsessively, I'd say: I spent the last 6 months chatting pretty much daily with ChatGPT (until it asked me to pay), then Claude (until it asked me to pay), then Gemini (who never asked me to pay), in one unique goal: make my dictionary fit in the smallest possible zip file, ideally under 12kb.

Six month of discussion about compressing a dictionary, that's long. How long? Well, just to give you an idea, I copy-pasted all my exchanges in a Word document with a 12pt font-size, and it exceeded 1500 pages.

Here's another way to see it: I made a video showing my conversation (in french) with the 3 AIs:

How did I brainstorm with these AIs:

As I said, I didn't ask the AIs to do everything for me. I did most of the thinking, and only sometimes, asked their help to formalize my ideas or suggest some improvements.
Every time I had a (more or less crazy) new idea to compress my words, I explained that idea to the AI and asked it to code me a little "HTML lab": a webpage that loads the dictionary, applies all the transformations I want, roadroll and zip the result, and check if the encoded data is 1) decodable without ambiguity, and 2) smaller that our current record.
So technically, these HTML labs are vibe-coded, but they're not part of a game, only tools used to generate a compressed dictionary.

I'm pretty sure we covered virtually every possible compression approach from woff2 to to arithmetic encoding, with long detours towards tries, RLE, SSE, ZSDT, DAWG, BWT, bigrams, trigrams, lexical pruning, column delta, and many others...

The main thing that I retained from the vast majority of these approaches is that they introduced entropy in my data, making it actually less RoadRoller-friendly and less Zip-friendly. Sad... But logic.

Since RoadRoller and Zip love low-entropy (repeated and/or easily predictable) data, the only viable solution was to transform my dictionary, but juuuuust a little:
emoving prefixes (of course, we saw earlier that it was beneficial), nudging little things here and there, reordering things, introducing a few special characters to help eliminate plenty of redundant information...
but never, never, making the data less predictable by trying to be "clever". No binary code, no random noise. When I understood that, my "dumb" suggestions finally became the "clever" ones, and ended up reducing my zip's size!

Starting from MiniPrefixRemover, the 5 best compressor improvements that we made during this long brainstorming were saved in my Wordcompression Github repo.

As I said, the AIs were mostly able to suggest well-known compression algorithms, but the "recipes" that we ended up using were 100% dictated by me. The AIs were there to formalize my ideas, implement the HTML labs, and explain to me why some things worked and some other things didn't work.
They've been a great help, without ruining the fun of thinking and "having my hands inside the engine" the whole time.
I really consider that it's how AIs should be used, for developers who want to keep enjoying making things!
